# Seasonal Adjustment of Inflation-Indexed Bond Yields — A Simple Model

When viewing the TIPS yield curve (yield vs. term to maturity), we typically see a sawtooth pattern that is more pronounced at shorter maturities, with yields of adjacent maturities above or below the general slope of the curve in that segment. These deviations from a smooth curve occur due to seasonal variation in the Consumer Price Index (CPI), which affects TIPS prices, and thus their yields. To determine the true financial merit of each of these TIPS, we need to separate the seasonal component from the trend component of the price index, calculate a seasonally adjusted price, and then calculate a seasonally adjusted yield.

To illustrate the how and why of this, we will develop a very simple model that retains only the elements that are required to understand it.

## The model

Our model has three points in time, t₀, t₁, and t₂. The interval from t₀ to t₁ is equal to the interval from t₁ to t₂; we call each interval a period, and the two periods together span one year, Y.

A price index measures the general price level at each point in time, as the Consumer Price Index does. We write the value of the price index at time t as Iₜ, and we set its starting value I₀ = 100.

Every bond in the model has a par amount (also called face amount) of 100, repaid at maturity. Treasuries use 1,000; we use 100 for simplicity. Price is quoted as a percent of par, so a bond issued at par has a price of 100. Interest accrues at the annual rate r and is paid with the par amount at maturity. The interest is 100 * r/2 for each half-year period and does not compound, so a bond held k periods repays 100 * (1 + k * r/2). The numeric examples use r = 0.02, so the interest is 100 * 0.02/2 = 1 per period, and a bond held k periods repays 100 * (1 + k * 0.01). The bond's value is its par amount plus the interest accrued so far. The chart below shows it as the gap above the par line.

The model has two kinds of bond, each issued at t₀ at par: a nominal bond and an inflation-indexed bond. A nominal bond repays the value shown above at maturity: 101 (NB1) at t₁, or 102 (NB2) at t₂. An inflation-indexed bond repays that same value multiplied by the price index ratio at maturity: IB1 repays 101 * (I₁ / I₀) at t₁, and IB2 repays 102 * (I₂ / I₀) at t₂.

## Scenario 1 — the price index does not change

The price index holds the value 100 at every date: I₀ = I₁ = I₂ = 100. Each ratio Iₜ / I₀ = 100 / 100 = 1, so IB1 repays the same as NB1 and IB2 the same as NB2. Every bond's value follows the interest-only path above, and every bond is priced at par with a yield equal to r = 0.02.

## Scenario 2 — a seasonal move in the price index

The price index starts at I₀ = 100. At t₀ it becomes known that I₁ = 101 and I₂ = 100: it rises by one over the first period and returns over the second, for no change across the year.

An inflation-indexed bond's value is its interest-only value multiplied by Iₜ / I₀. The nominal bonds keep to the interest-only path, 100 → 101 → 102. The indexed bonds follow that path multiplied by the price index ratio: 100 at t₀, then 101 * (I₁/I₀) = 101 * (101/100) = 102.01 at t₁, then 102 * (I₂/I₀) = 102 * (100/100) = 102 at t₂.

IB2 matures at t₂, where I₂ / I₀ = 100 / 100 = 1, so IB2 repays 102 * 1 = 102, the same as NB2; its price stays at 100 and its yield at r = 0.02.

IB1 matures at t₁, where I₁ / I₀ = 101 / 100 = 1.01, so IB1 repays 101 * 1.01 = 102.01, more than the 101 NB1 repays. An investor can borrow and lend at rate r: 100 borrowed at t₀ is repaid as 100 * (1 + r/2) = 101 at t₁. An investor who borrows 100, buys IB1 at 100, and holds it to t₁ then receives 102.01, repays 101, and keeps 1.01, with nothing committed and no risk. This is an arbitrage: at a price of 100, IB1 is too cheap.

At a price of 100, demand for IB1 exceeds supply, and the price rises. It rises until the arbitrage is gone: the loan needed to buy IB1, repaid at t₁, then equals IB1's repayment. P * (1 + r/2) = 102.01, so P * 1.01 = 102.01, so P = 101. A bond's yield y is computed from its price P and its par-plus-interest repayment M = 100 * (1 + n * r/2), with n the number of periods to maturity: M = P * (1 + n * y/2). The price index ratio is not part of M. For IB1, P = 101, n = 1, M = 100 * (1 + 0.01) = 101:

    101 = 101 * (1 + y/2)
    y = 0

against r = 0.02 for every other bond. IB1's quoted price of 101 is its par value 100 multiplied by the price index ratio I₁ / I₀ = 101/100 = 1.01. Its yield is computed from this quoted price and its par-plus-interest repayment, 101 — the same calculation used for every bond, and it does not reference the price index. Because the price carries the price index ratio and the repayment used in the calculation does not, the computed yield is 0, below r = 0.02.
