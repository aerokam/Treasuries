# Seasonal Adjustment of Inflation-Indexed Bond Yields — A Simple Model

When viewing the TIPS yield curve (yield vs. term to maturity), we typically see a sawtooth pattern that is more pronounced at shorter maturities, with yields of adjacent maturities above or below the general slope of the curve in that segment. These deviations from a smooth curve occur due to seasonal variation in the Consumer Price Index (CPI), which affects TIPS prices, and thus their yields. To determine the true financial merit of each of these TIPS, we need to separate the seasonal component from the trend component of the price index, calculate a seasonally adjusted price, and then calculate a seasonally adjusted yield.

To illustrate the how and why of this, we will develop a very simple model that retains only the elements that are required to understand it.

## The model

Our model has three points in time: t = 0, t = 0.5, and t = 1.0, in years. The interval from 0 to 0.5 equals the interval from 0.5 to 1.0; we call each interval a period, and together they span one year. From here we write each date by its number of years: 0, 0.5, or 1.

A price index measures the general price level at each point in time, as the Consumer Price Index does. We write its value at time t as I(t), and set its starting value I(0) = 100.

Every bond in the model has a par amount (also called face amount) of 100, repaid at maturity. Treasuries use 1,000; we use 100 for simplicity. Price is quoted as a percent of par, so a bond issued at par has a price of 100. Interest accrues at the annual rate r and does not compound, so a bond's par-plus-interest value at time t is

    V(t) = 100 * (1 + t * r)

We write r as a percentage: the numeric examples use r = 2.0%, chosen so each half-year period adds 100 * r/2 = 100 * 1.0% = 1 to the value: V(0) = 100, V(0.5) = 101, V(1) = 102. V(t) is a bond's par amount plus the interest accrued by time t; the chart below shows it as the gap above the par line.

The model has two kinds of bond, each issued at 0 at par: a nominal bond and an inflation-indexed bond. A nominal bond repays V(t) at maturity: V(0.5) = 101 (NB1), or V(1) = 102 (NB2). An inflation-indexed bond repays that value multiplied by the price index ratio at maturity: IB1 repays V(0.5) * (I(0.5) / I(0)), and IB2 repays V(1) * (I(1) / I(0)).

A bond's yield is the discount rate that equates its price today to the present value of its repayment at maturity: price equals the repayment discounted at the yield. With t years to maturity and an annual yield rate y, that is

    Price = Repayment / (1 + t * y)

or, rearranged to solve for y once the price and repayment are known,

    Repayment = Price * (1 + t * y)

A bond's repayment, before any price index adjustment, is its par-plus-interest value, V(t). For a bond priced at par, Price = V(0) = 100, so V(t) = 100 * (1 + t * y); since V(t) = 100 * (1 + t * r) by definition, this gives y = r for any bond priced at par.

## Scenario 1 — the price index does not change

The price index holds the value 100 at every date: I(0) = I(0.5) = I(1) = 100. Each ratio I(t) / I(0) = 1, so IB1 repays the same as NB1 and IB2 the same as NB2 — every bond is priced at par, 100, and by the yield formula above, every bond's yield is r = 2.0%.

## Scenario 2 — a seasonal move in the price index

The price index starts at I(0) = 100. At t = 0 it becomes known that I(0.5) = 101 and I(1) = 100: it increases by 1 unit over the first period and decreases by 1 unit over the second — the same scale as the interest accrual — for no change across the year.

An inflation-indexed bond's value is V(t) multiplied by I(t) / I(0). The nominal bonds keep to V(t): 100 → 101 → 102. The indexed bonds follow V(t) multiplied by the price index ratio: 100 at t = 0, then V(0.5) * (I(0.5)/I(0)) = 101 * 1.01 = 102.01 at t = 0.5, then V(1) * (I(1)/I(0)) = 102 * 1.00 = 102 at t = 1.

IB2 matures at t = 1, where I(1) / I(0) = 100/100 = 1, so IB2 repays V(1) * 1 = 102, the same as NB2, and its price stays at 100. Priced at par, its yield is r = 2.0%.

IB1 matures at t = 0.5, where I(0.5) / I(0) = 101/100 = 1.01, so IB1 repays V(0.5) * 1.01 = 101 * 1.01 = 102.01, more than the 101 NB1 repays. An investor can borrow and lend at rate r; borrowing 100 at t = 0 is repaid as V(0.5) = 101 at t = 0.5. An investor who borrows 100, buys IB1 at 100, and holds it to t = 0.5 then receives 102.01, repays 101, and keeps 1.01, with nothing committed and no risk. This is an arbitrage: at a price of 100, IB1 is too cheap.

Assume the supply of each bond is fixed. At a price of 100, IB1 offers a larger risk-free gain than NB1, so demand for IB1 exceeds demand for NB1, and IB1's price rises. It rises until the arbitrage is gone: the loan needed to buy IB1, repaid at t = 0.5, then equals IB1's repayment.

    Price * (1 + 0.5 * r) = 102.01
    Price * 1.01 = 102.01
    Price = 101

For IB1, the repayment used in the yield calculation is V(0.5) = 101 (before any price index adjustment), its price is 101, and it matures at t = 0.5. By the yield formula above:

    101 = 101 * (1 + 0.5 * y)
    1 = 1 + 0.5 * y
    0 = 0.5 * y
    y = 0.0%

against r = 2.0% for every other bond. IB1's price, 101, is its par value 100 multiplied by the price index ratio I(0.5) / I(0) = 101/100 = 1.01. Because that ratio raised the price but not the repayment used to compute yield, IB1's yield comes out below r — here, all the way to 0.0%.
